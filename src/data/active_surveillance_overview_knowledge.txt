Active Surveillance and Observation in Prostate Cancer

A Patient Guide Based on NCCN, AUA, and EAU Guidelines

What These Options Mean

Active Surveillance (AS):

A structured monitoring program for men with very-low or low-risk prostate cancer. The goal is to
watch the cancer closely with tests and repeat biopsies, and only begin treatment if the disease
shows signs of growth or change.

Observation (Watchful Waiting):

A less intensive approach, usually chosen by men with limited life expectancy or major health
problems. The goal is to avoid unnecessary testing and treatments, focusing instead on comfort and
quality of life. Treatment is only given if symptoms develop.

Key Point: Neither AS nor observation is a guarantee. Tests can miss cancer or underestimate
aggressiveness. These approaches balance benefits and risks but cannot eliminate risk entirely.

Shared Decision-Making and Quality of Life
What this means for you

Choosing a treatment for prostate cancer is not just about the cancer itself. It's also about how each
option could affect your everyday life. Shared decision-making means you and your doctor decide
together, based on the cancer, your health, and what matters most to you.

What we will talk about together

1. How serious your cancer is
We will explain your risk level and what that means for your care.

2. Your overall health and life expectancy
Age and other health conditions help guide whether treatment or careful monitoring makes more
sense.

3. Chances of cure and recurrence
Tools like risk calculators can help estimate how likely treatment is to cure the cancer or whether
the cancer might return.

4. Possible side effects
Every treatment can cause side effects, especially affecting urinary control, sexual function, and
bowel health. These can be temporary or long-lasting.

6. Your starting point
Your current urinary, sexual, and bowel function is one of the best predictors of your recovery. We
may use questionnaires (like SHIM, IPSS) to record this.

6. Your preferences and goals
What matters most to you, whether it’s keeping sexual function, avoiding incontinence, or limiting
side effects, should guide the choice.

Psychological and Emotional Aspects

A. Anxiety before the procedure

+ Many men feel nervous about the biopsy itself, the discomfort, and the possible results.
«  Itis normal to feel worried about cancer, even when the biopsy is only being done as a
precaution.

B. Concerns about outcomes

+ Waiting for results can be stressful and may cause sleep problems, irritability, or constant
worry.

* Talking openly with your doctor about what the results could mean may help reduce fear of the
unknown.

C. Impact on self-image and intimacy

» Some men worry about sexual function, masculinity, or how the biopsy might affect intimacy.
e Itmay help to share these concerns with your partner and your healthcare team.

D. Coping strategies

« Stay informed: Ask your doctor to explain each step so you know what to expect.

¢ Use support: Talk with loved ones, a counselor, or support groups for men facing prostate
cancer evaluations.

- Practice stress relief. Deep breathing, light exercise, or mindfulness can ease anxiety.

Who May Be a Candidate

Active Surveillance is generally considered when:

Prostate cancer is very-low or low risk (Grade Group 1, PSA <10, only a few biopsy cores
positive).
Life expectancy is 10+ years.
The patient is comfortable with frequent monitoring and possible later treatment.
Sometimes considered in favorable intermediate-risk cases if:

o Only a small amount of Gleason pattern 4 is present.

o PSA density is low.

o Tumor volume is low.

o Genomic tests suggest low risk.

Observation is considered when:

Cancer is low- or intermediate-risk, but life expectancy is <5 years.
The risks of ongoing testing and treatment outweigh the benefits.
The focus of care is comfort and minimizing interventions.

Note: Men with family history or known inherited mutations (e.g., BRCA2, HOXB13) may be less
suitable for surveillance and often need closer follow-up.

Special Pathologic Findings and Active Surveillance

Certain biopsy findings may raise concern for higher-risk disease. While guidelines and literature
often suggest these features should not be managed with active surveillance, there may be
individualized situations where surveillance is still considered after a detailed discussion with your
healthcare team:

Cribriform carcinoma — often associated with higher rates of progression, metastasis, and
mortality.

Intraductal carcinoma of the prostate (IDC-P) — considered an adverse histologic finding,
typically prompting definitive treatment.

High-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (HGPIN) — not prostate cancer itself, but a
marker of increased risk. Requires repeat biopsy rather than surveillance.

Atypical small acinar proliferation (ASAP / ASIN) — an indeterminate finding with a high
likelihood of underlying carcinoma. Repeat biopsy is recommended within 3-6 months.
Other aggressive histologies — such as ductal adenocarcinoma, sarcomatoid carcinoma, or
small cell/neuroendocrine carcinoma, are not candidates for surveillance.

Note: Many clinical guidelines (NCCN, AUA, EAU) suggest that these features usually warrant
treatment rather than surveillance because of their aggressive potential. However, management is
individualized. In select cases, a patient and their doctor may still choose surveillance, but this
requires careful monitoring and shared decision-making.

Confirming Candidacy

Because biopsies can underestimate cancer, confirmatory testing is strongly recommended before
committing to AS:

¢ Repeat biopsy (within 12-24 months of diagnosis).
e MRI with PSA density to identify hidden higher-grade disease.
« Genomic testing (Decipher, Oncotype DX, Prolaris, ConfirmMDx) may add information.

Note: Even with these tests, aggressive cancer can sometimes remain undetected.

What Active Surveillance Involves
Typical monitoring schedule (may be individualized):

PSA test: About every 6 months.

Digital rectal exam (DRE): About once per year.

Prostate biopsy: Every 2-5 years, but not more than once per year unless concerns arise.
MRI: Sometimes repeated (not more than once per year) to guide biopsies, but cannot replace
them.

Surveillance continues until life expectancy is less than 10 years, at which point observation may be
more appropriate.

When Surveillance May Change to Treatment
Curative treatment may be discussed if:

Biopsy shows higher grade disease (Grade Group 22).

Cancer volume increases significantly.

PSA density rises in a concerning way.

MRI shows new or suspicious lesions.

The patient experiences significant anxiety or prefers treatment despite stable disease.

Note: Sometimes cancer progresses despite stable tests, and sometimes PSA rises without
progression. Decisions are individualized.

Advantages of Active Surveillance

° 50-68% of men avoid treatment for at least 10 years.
¢ Preserves quality of life by delaying urinary, sexual, and bowel side effects.
e Curative treatment is still available if disease changes.

Limitations of Active Surveillance

» 32-50% of men eventually need treatment within 10 years.
« Small risk (<0.5%) of progression to advanced cancer during surveillance.
* Some men experience anxiety about “living with cancer.”

Disclaimer: Choosing surveillance means accepting both the benefits of avoiding overtreatment and
the risks of possible disease progression.

Observation (Watchful Waiting)

Observation focuses on comfort, not routine testing. Usually chosen if life expectancy is <5 years or
other health problems outweigh cancer risk.

Follow-up:

« Occasional clinic visits and physical exams (about once a year).
¢ Treatment begins only if symptoms develop (e.g., urinary blockage, bone pain, weight loss).

Note: With observation, cancer may cause symptoms without PSA warning. Promptly report new or
conceming symptoms to your doctor.

Role of Additional Tests

« PSA: Can be influenced by non-cancer causes (infection, inflammation, recent ejaculation).
Abnormal results should be confirmed.

« MRI: Improves detection of higher-grade disease but can miss cancer. Radiologist skill matters.

« PSMA PET: Very sensitive for detecting spread; sometimes used in higher-risk cases. Not a
substitute for biopsy.

« ExactVu Micro-Ultrasound: Provides high-resolution imaging and may help guide targeted
biopsies. Not a replacement for surveillance biopsies.

+ Genomic Testing: Helps guide initial candidacy. Not usually repeated during AS.

Survivorship and Long-Term Care

Men on AS or observation benefit from:

Regular follow-up to ensure safety.

Lifestyle modifications (diet, exercise, smoking cessation) to reduce risk.

Emotional support through counseling or support groups.

Coordination of care among urologists, oncologists, primary care, and mental health providers.

Bottom Line

¢ Active surveillance is recommended for most men with very-low and low-risk prostate cancer and
a life expectancy of 10 years or more.

Observation is best for men with shorter life expectancy or significant comorbidities.

Both strategies reduce unnecessary treatment but carry risks of missed or progressive disease.
No approach guarantees cure, stability, or complete safety.

The best choice depends on your risk level, overall health, and personal values, guided by a
shared decision-making discussion with your doctor.
